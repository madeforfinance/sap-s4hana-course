Retained Earnings Account & Ledger Creation

In Lesson 7 you built the Chart of Accounts — the full list of G/L accounts. But two things are still missing before SAP can ever close a year or store a posting properly. First: when the year ends, where does the profit or loss go? That answer is the Retained Earnings Account. Second: every posting in SAP is written into a book called a Ledger — and in this lesson you create that book. (Is lesson mein hum saal ke aakhir mein munafa/nuksan ko band karna, aur SAP ki asal "bahi khata" — ledger — banana seekhenge.)

01 Where we are in the course

A quick recap, so this lesson sits in the right place in your head.

So far you have built the skeleton of a company inside SAP:

  • Lesson 5 — Company and Company Code (the legal unit that keeps its own books).
  • Lesson 6 — Business Area, Fiscal Year Variant (the calendar of the books), Credit Control Area, Posting Period Variant (which months are open for posting).
  • Lesson 7 — Currencies, the Chart of Accounts (the list of all G/L accounts, T-code OB13), and Field Status.

Now think like an accountant for one second. Your chart of accounts has two families of accounts:

Balance Sheet accounts

Cash, bank, machinery, loans, capital. These live on — their balance on 31 December becomes the opening balance on 1 January. (Ye khaate agle saal mein bhi chalte rehte hain.)

P&L accounts (Income & Expense)

Sales, rent, salaries, electricity. These are only for one year. At year-end they must be closed to zero, and their net result — profit or loss — must move somewhere. That "somewhere" is today's first topic.

New term — G/L Account

G/L means General Ledger. A G/L account is one head of account in the chart of accounts — for example "Rent Expense" or "Bank Account". You already know this from manual accounting: it is simply one page of your ledger book.

02 Year-end in plain accounting — first, no SAP

You already know this from Financial Accounting. SAP only automates it.

At the end of the year, an accountant prepares the Profit & Loss Account (Income Statement). All revenues are collected on one side, all expenses on the other. The difference is:

  • Revenue > Expense → Net Profit
  • Expense > Revenue → Net Loss

That net profit or loss does not disappear. It belongs to the owners of the business, so it is transferred to the Balance Sheet, into the owners' equity section. Year after year, these undistributed profits keep collecting in one equity account. Accountants call that accumulated amount Retained Earnings — the profit the business retained (kept inside the business) instead of paying out as dividend.

New term — Retained Earnings

The total profit of all past years, minus losses and minus dividends paid out. It is a Balance Sheet account sitting inside Equity (owners' funds), next to Share Capital. (Saalon ka jama shuda munafa jo business ne bahar nahi nikala, andar hi rakha.)

Analogy — the savings jar

Imagine a shopkeeper who, every December, counts the year's profit and puts it into one big glass jar labelled "Savings". He never mixes it with daily cash. After 5 years, the jar shows the total savings of 5 years. That jar is Retained Earnings: every year's result is poured into the same one jar, and it keeps growing (or shrinking, in a loss year).

Memory trick

"P&L dies every year, Retained Earnings lives forever." Income and expense accounts are reborn empty each year; the retained earnings account is the one permanent home of every year's result.

03 The Retained Earnings Account in SAP

SAP cannot close the year unless you first tell it: "this is the jar."

In SAP, the retained earnings account is a normal Balance Sheet G/L account — for example account 330000 "Retained Earnings" — that you create in the chart of accounts like any other account (T-code FS00, from Lesson 7). Creating the account alone is not enough. You must also declare it as the retained earnings account, so SAP knows: "when the year closes, pour every P&L result into this account."

The link: P&L Statement Account Type

How does SAP know which P&L accounts belong to which retained earnings account? Through a small code called the P&L statement account type.

New term — P&L Statement Account Type

A one-character code (standard SAP uses X) that works like a label. You stick this label on (1) the retained earnings account definition, and (2) every P&L account in the chart of accounts. At closing time, SAP collects all P&L accounts carrying label X and transfers their total to the retained earnings account that also carries label X.

Where is the label set?

  • On each P&L account: in FS00, in the chart of accounts segment (the part of the account valid for the whole chart — table SKA1), there is a field "P&L statement account type". For revenue and expense accounts you enter X there.
  • On the retained earnings side: in T-code OB53 you say "for chart of accounts YCOA, type X means account 330000".
Analogy — labelled boxes and one shelf

Think of a warehouse. Every income/expense box gets a sticker "X". One shelf is marked: "All X boxes are emptied onto THIS shelf — shelf 330000." At year-end, the worker (SAP's carryforward program) simply follows the stickers. No sticker, no transfer — that account would be forgotten at closing. (Jis khaate par X ka label nahi, wo saal-band mein reh jayega.)

One retained earnings account — or more?

The standard, and what the exam expects, is one retained earnings account per chart of accounts, using type X. But SAP technically allows more than one: you can define extra types (for example A, B) each pointing to a different retained earnings account — useful if a group wants, say, operating profit and financial profit accumulated separately. Each P&L account then carries the type of the retained earnings account it should close into.

Exam trap #1

The retained earnings account is defined per Chart of Accounts — NOT per Company Code. The OB53 screen asks for the chart of accounts key, never a company code. Because many company codes can share one chart (Lesson 7), they automatically share the same retained earnings definition. If an exam option says "retained earnings is defined for each company code in OB53" — it is wrong.

Exam trap #2

You never post directly to the retained earnings account in daily business, and it is a Balance Sheet account, not a P&L account. Its balance changes only through the year-end balance carryforward (and rare manual corrections by senior consultants). If a question calls it a "P&L account" — wrong family: it lives in Equity on the Balance Sheet.

04 Define it in OB53 — step by step

Two minutes of configuration that the whole year-end depends on.

Before you start — the prerequisites

  • The chart of accounts exists (Lesson 7, OB13) — here we use YCOA.
  • The retained earnings G/L account itself exists in that chart — e.g. 330000, created as a Balance Sheet account.
  • Your P&L accounts have (or will have) X in their P&L statement account type field.

The steps

  • Step 1 — Open T-code OB53, or follow the SPRO path below.
  • Step 2 — SAP asks for the Chart of Accounts. Enter YCOA and press Enter.
  • Step 3 — Choose New Entries. In the P&L statement account type column enter X.
  • Step 4 — In the Account column enter the retained earnings G/L account number, e.g. 330000.
  • Step 5 — Save. SAP asks for a transport request (Lesson 2 — the parcel that carries configuration from DEV to QAS to PRD). Confirm it.
New term — SPRO

SPRO is the T-code that opens SAP's configuration menu — the big tree of settings screens where consultants set up the system. Almost every "define" screen in this course can be reached either by its direct T-code (like OB53) or by walking the SPRO tree. The SPRO path for this setting is:
SPRO → Financial Accounting → General Ledger Accounting → Master Data → G/L Accounts → Preparations → Define Retained Earnings Account

That is the entire configuration. One row: X → 330000. Behind the scenes SAP stores it in table T030 (the big configuration table for standard account determination — you will meet it again in later topics).

Memory trick

"OB53 — the year-end's address." When the carryforward program asks "where should the profit go?", OB53 is the address book it opens. And remember X marks the spot — type X is the standard label, like an X on a treasure map pointing to the retained earnings account.

05 Balance Carryforward — how the closing actually runs

OB53 only writes the address. This is the program that delivers the parcel. C_TS4FI favourite

New term — Balance Carryforward

The year-end job that moves balances from the closing fiscal year into the new fiscal year. In S/4HANA you run it with T-code FAGLGVTR. (In old ECC the transaction was F.16 — the exam is on S/4HANA, so answer FAGLGVTR.)

The carryforward treats the two account families differently — this difference is a classic exam question:

Account familyWhat FAGLGVTR does
Balance Sheet accountsTheir closing balance is carried forward as the opening balance of the new year — account by account, exactly as it was.
P&L accountsThey are closed to zero. Their total net result is posted to the Retained Earnings account (found through the type, e.g. X, in OB53). In the new year every P&L account starts at zero, and Retained Earnings shows the accumulated result.

Important details the exam loves

  • It is done per company code and per ledger — you select the company code(s), the ledger, and the new fiscal year when running FAGLGVTR. (Ledgers are Part B of this lesson — hold on for two minutes.)
  • It can be re-run. If late postings land in the old year afterwards, you simply run the carryforward again; SAP updates the carried-forward amounts. It is not a one-time, point-of-no-return event.
  • No journal document is posted for the P&L closing. The transfer to retained earnings happens inside the carryforward — the P&L accounts just start the new year at zero with their result sitting in Retained Earnings. Do not imagine a manual "Profit Dr / Retained Earnings Cr" entry in FB50.
  • Order matters: if the retained earnings account is not defined in OB53, or a P&L account is missing its type X, the carryforward cannot place that result correctly — configuration first (this lesson), closing later.
Analogy — shifting to a new register

A shopkeeper finishes the year's register book. Furniture, stock and cash-in-hand figures are copied as-is onto page 1 of the new book (Balance Sheet accounts). But the daily income/expense pages are not copied — only their final total, "this year we earned Rs 5 lakh", is written into the savings jar page (Retained Earnings), and the income/expense pages of the new book start blank. That copying job is FAGLGVTR.

06 Part B — What is a Ledger in SAP?

Video 05:41:00. First the accounting word, then the SAP word — they are cousins, not twins.

In your accounting studies, the ledger is the main book of accounts: every journal entry is posted into ledger accounts, and from the ledger you prepare the trial balance and financial statements. In SAP the word is used in a very specific technical way:

New term — Ledger (SAP sense)

A ledger in SAP is a complete, separate set of books inside the General Ledger — its own full copy of postings, kept under one set of accounting rules. One company code can keep several ledgers at the same time: one for local law, one for group reporting rules, and so on. Every posting knows which ledger(s) it belongs to.

Why would one company need more than one set of books? Because different rule-makers ask for different numbers:

  • Local law / tax rules — e.g. Pakistani companies reporting under local requirements.
  • Group rules (IFRS) — a multinational parent wants every subsidiary's numbers under IFRS.
  • The same purchase of a machine can be depreciated one way for local books and another way for group books. Two rule sets → two valuations → parallel accounting.
New term — Parallel Accounting

Keeping books under two or more accounting principles at the same time in the same system. (Ek hi company, do alag qawaid ke hisaab — ek saath.) The main way to do this in S/4HANA is the ledger approach: one ledger per accounting principle.

Analogy — one match, two scorebooks

Two scorekeepers watch the same cricket match. One writes the scorebook in the local style, the other in the international style. Same match, same balls — but two complete, separate scorebooks, each correct under its own rules. A ledger is a scorebook: same business events, recorded under one accounting principle.

07 The Leading Ledger — 0L

Every SAP system has exactly one boss ledger. It arrives pre-delivered; you do not create it.

New term — Leading Ledger

The main ledger of the system. SAP delivers it with the fixed key 0L (zero-L). There is exactly one leading ledger in the whole client, and it is the ledger of the main accounting principle — normally the one used for the company's primary financial statements.

What makes 0L special?

  • It receives every posting. When a user posts any normal document and does not name a ledger, the posting goes into the leading ledger (and into any non-leading ledgers in parallel — Section 8). You can never post "around" it.
  • It is the only ledger connected to Controlling (CO). Management accounting reads its actuals from the leading ledger.
  • Company codes are assigned to it, and each company code takes its most important settings from the leading ledger's assignment: the local currency (Lesson 7), and the fiscal-year / posting-period settings come from the company code's variants (Lesson 6) as maintained for the leading ledger.
  • It always exists. 0L comes with the standard system. Your job in configuration is not to create it, but to check its settings and assign company codes.
New term — Non-Leading Ledger

Any ledger other than 0L. You create these yourself, with your own two-character keys (for example 2L, 3L, or letters like IF). Each non-leading ledger represents one additional accounting principle (for example IFRS for group reporting) and can have its own fiscal year variant and its own currencies, different from the leading ledger.

Memory trick

"0L = Zero choice, Leader always." The leading ledger's key starts with 0 because it is the first and only boss — and L for Leader. One Leader, many followers (non-leading ledgers).

08 Create a Non-Leading Ledger — step by step

The video's task: create a ledger for the second set of books. In S/4HANA this is done in one modern transaction.

New term — FINSC_LEDGER

The S/4HANA configuration transaction for ledgers. One screen family where you define ledgers, mark the leading ledger, and make the company code settings for the ledger (currencies, fiscal year variant, posting period variant per company code). The SPRO path is:
SPRO → Financial Accounting → Financial Accounting Global Settings → Ledgers → Ledger → Define Ledgers for General Ledger Accounting

The steps (as in the video)

  • Step 1 — Open FINSC_LEDGER (or follow the SPRO path above). You see the list of existing ledgers — 0L is already there, flagged as Leading.
  • Step 2 — Choose New Entries and create your ledger: enter a 2-character key, e.g. 2L, and a description, e.g. "IFRS Ledger". Leave the Leading checkbox empty — only 0L may carry it.
  • Step 3 — Select your new ledger and go to "Company Code Settings for the Ledger". Add your company code, e.g. PK01.
  • Step 4 — For that company code + ledger combination, set:
    • Local currency — normally the same as the company code currency (Lesson 7);
    • Optionally a second (parallel) currency, e.g. a group currency like USD;
    • Fiscal Year Variant — can be the same as the leading ledger's (e.g. K4) or a different one if this rule book follows a different calendar;
    • Posting Period Variant — controls which periods are open for this ledger (Lesson 6).
  • Step 5 — Save (with transport request). The ledger is now active for that company code.

What happens after creation — how postings flow

  • A normal posting (no ledger named) is written into the leading ledger and all assigned non-leading ledgers at the same time — one document, several ledger views.
  • An adjustment only for one principle (e.g. extra IFRS depreciation) is posted to a specific ledger using a ledger group — next section. It changes only that ledger's books; the leading ledger never sees it.
  • Each ledger keeps its own balances and its own balance carryforward (remember FAGLGVTR is run per ledger — now that detail makes sense).
Exam trap #3

You cannot create a second leading ledger, and you cannot make your new ledger "leading" while 0L exists. Exactly one leading ledger per client — an exam option offering two leading ledgers is always wrong.

Exam trap #4

Defining the ledger alone does nothing. The ledger works for a company code only after the company code settings for that ledger are maintained (currency, fiscal year variant, posting period variant). "Ledger defined but company code not assigned → no postings in that ledger" is a favourite scenario question.

09 Ledger Groups & Parallel Accounting — completing the picture

How do you post to just one ledger? You aim the document with a group.

New term — Ledger Group

A named bundle of ledgers used as the target of a posting. When you create a ledger, SAP automatically creates a ledger group with the same name containing only that ledger. So to post an IFRS-only adjustment, you enter ledger group 2L in the document — the posting lands only in ledger 2L. Posting without any ledger group hits all ledgers.

New term — Representative Ledger

In a ledger group that contains several ledgers, one ledger is marked the representative ledger. Its posting period check decides whether posting is allowed: if the period is open in the representative ledger, the document may post to the group. For the automatic one-ledger groups, that single ledger is its own representative.

Ledger approach vs Accounts approach

There are two classic designs for parallel accounting. S/4HANA strongly prefers the first — and the exam knows it:

Ledger ApproachAccounts Approach
IdeaOne chart of accounts; separate ledger per accounting principleOne ledger; extra G/L accounts per principle inside the same books
Where the difference livesIn the ledger (0L vs 2L)In different account numbers
S/4HANA statusRecommended / standardLegacy approach from older systems
Analogy — two notebooks vs one notebook, two pens

Ledger approach: two separate notebooks, one per rule set — clean, each book tells one complete story. Accounts approach: one notebook, but some pages written with a second pen for the second rule set — the totals mix together and are hard to separate. SAP's advice: separate notebooks. (Har qaaida ki apni copy — yehi asaan aur saaf tareeqa hai.)

One more connection — where do ledger postings physically live?

Every G/L document line in S/4HANA is stored in the Universal Journal table ACDOCA, and every line carries its ledger field (RLDNR) — that is how one table can hold many ledgers' books at once. The Universal Journal gets its own full treatment in Lesson 9; for today, just remember: ledger is a field on every journal line, and the leading ledger's lines are the ones CO reads.

Memory trick

"No group named → all ledgers fed. Group named → only those ledgers fed." And for creation order: Define the Ledger, then give it a Company Code home — a ledger without company-code settings is a book with no shelf.

11 Exam Traps — read these twice

Traps 1–4 appeared inside the sections. Here is the complete set for Lesson 8.

Trap 1 — Level of definition

Retained earnings is defined per chart of accounts (OB53), never per company code.

Trap 2 — Wrong account family

Retained Earnings is a Balance Sheet / Equity account. Any option calling it a P&L account, or saying users post daily entries to it, is wrong.

Trap 3 — Two leading ledgers

Impossible. One leading ledger per client — always 0L, delivered by SAP.

Trap 4 — Ledger without company code settings

A defined ledger with no company code settings receives no postings for that company code. Definition + assignment — both steps are needed.

Trap 5 — "Creating" 0L

You never create the leading ledger; you only create non-leading ledgers. A step-by-step option that starts with "create ledger 0L" is a distractor.

Trap 6 — Carryforward posts a document

FAGLGVTR does not post a normal journal document for the P&L closing; P&L accounts open the new year at zero and their result sits in Retained Earnings. It can also be re-run after late postings.

Trap 7 — Posting to one ledger only

A ledger-specific posting is made with a ledger group, not by "switching off" other ledgers. No ledger group in the document = the posting goes to all ledgers.

Trap 8 — Only one retained earnings account can ever exist

Standard practice is one (type X), but SAP allows multiple retained earnings accounts per chart using different P&L statement account types. "Only one is technically possible" is false.

Trap 9 — Non-leading ledger and CO

Only the leading ledger is integrated with Controlling. Non-leading ledgers are FI-only views.

13 Summary — Lesson 8 in one page

  • At year-end, P&L accounts close to zero; their net result moves into Retained Earnings, a Balance Sheet (equity) account holding all years' undistributed profit.
  • In SAP, the link is the P&L statement account type — standard X — set on every P&L account in FS00 (chart segment, SKA1), and mapped to one account in OB53 per chart of accounts (stored in T030). Multiple retained earnings accounts are possible with different types.
  • The closing job is the balance carryforward, FAGLGVTR — per company code and per ledger, re-runnable, no journal document posted for the P&L transfer.
  • A ledger is a complete set of books under one accounting principle. The leading ledger 0L is delivered by SAP, is unique, receives every posting, and is the only ledger linked to CO.
  • Non-leading ledgers (e.g. 2L for IFRS) are created in FINSC_LEDGER; they work only after company code settings (currency, fiscal year variant, posting period variant) are maintained.
  • Postings with no ledger group go to all ledgers; postings with a ledger group go only to that group's ledgers — the basis of parallel accounting (ledger approach, preferred in S/4HANA).
